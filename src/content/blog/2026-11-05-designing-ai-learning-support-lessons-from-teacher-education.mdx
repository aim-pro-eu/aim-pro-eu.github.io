*Three iSQD strategies for teacher learning: evaluating AI outputs, negotiating roles and co-creating with AI.*

What helps people move beyond a one-off introduction to AI? How can they develop the understanding and judgement to make deliberate choices about its use? Recent [publications on teacher education](https://aim-pro.eu/publications/) offer lessons for AIM-PRO. These lessons inform the design of tools and training that will be tested with users from higher education, vocational education and training (VET), and small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs).

### Training teachers

Preparing teachers for AI takes more than a one-off session. To familiarise them with the technology, we first build understanding, then consider what AI means for a particular subject and teaching context, and then explore its use. AI in language teaching raises different questions from AI in mathematics or history. As the technology evolves, teachers need ongoing professional learning, supported by colleagues and the wider organisation. This support should include safe, guided opportunities to practise, helping teachers work through uncertainty.

Teachers also need to negotiate their role alongside AI. For example, a teacher might use AI to suggest changes to an existing lesson, then assess and adapt those suggestions to their students’ needs. This means deciding what to delegate and where human judgement remains essential, while critically evaluating AI outputs for limitations and potential bias.

Our [July newsletter](https://aim-pro.eu/newsletters/2026-07-newsletter) explored these strategies and the research behind them. Bringing these lessons into tool design requires educational and technical expertise, alongside input from the people who will use the tools.


### Designing and testing across contexts

AIM-PRO brings educational and computer-science perspectives together, with contributions from the Vrije Universiteit Brussel and the University of Leeds alongside technical partners. This exchange was reflected in the recent keynote in Rennes, given jointly by the Vrije Universiteit Brussel and the University of L’Aquila.
Using realistic personas and context-specific scenarios with prospective users can help test whether the problems and proposed support resonate with teachers, trainers and professionals.
In each setting, we want to answer three core questions: can people use the tool, does it fit their work, and does it help them learn? We should also ask whether the design helps users judge when to trust AI and preserves human agency, and what personalisation and institutional support users need.
Of these questions, whether the tool helps people learn deserves particular attention: when AI helps someone produce a better result, does it also improve their own understanding and judgement?
For AIM-PRO, we design from research grounded in people’s experiences, then test with users whether the tools fit their context and help them learn.
